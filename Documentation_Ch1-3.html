CHAPTER ONE: INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background of the Study

Lecture timetabling is one of the most critical administrative functions in every institution of higher learning. It involves the allocation of courses, lecturers, classrooms, and time slots into a structured weekly schedule that avoids conflicts and satisfies a wide range of institutional constraints. In most universities, especially within the developing world, this process is performed manually by timetabling officers or departmental secretaries who rely on spreadsheets, paper-based charts, or basic word-processing tools to assemble schedules (Abdullah & Sahib, 2021).

The manual approach, while historically adequate for small institutions with few programmes, becomes progressively error-prone and inefficient as the number of academic programmes, departments, courses, and lecturers grows. A single university may offer hundreds of courses across dozens of programmes, each with its own level requirements, credit-hour allocations, room-type preferences (lecture halls vs. laboratories), and lecturer availability constraints. Coordinating all of these variables by hand frequently results in scheduling clashes where two courses are assigned the same room at the same time, or the same lecturer is expected to teach in two locations simultaneously (Bakir, 2023).

Furthermore, manual timetabling is inherently slow. Changes such as a lecturer calling in sick, a room undergoing maintenance, or a new course being added to the curriculum can require hours of reworking an entire semester's schedule. The lack of automation also means that there is no rapid way to regenerate a schedule from scratch when the underlying constraints change significantly from one academic session to the next.

With the rapid adoption of web technologies and cloud computing, universities are increasingly looking towards automated Timetable Generation Systems (TGS) to reduce the time spent on scheduling, minimize human error, and allow administrators to focus on higher-value tasks. Modern web-based systems can enforce hard constraints (no room or lecturer clashes) and soft constraints (preference for certain time slots or rooms), while producing multiple schedule variants in seconds (Edwards & Kudelska, 2022).

This project, the Automated Lecture Timetable Generation System (LecSched), is motivated by the need to replace the manual, error-prone scheduling practices observed at the institution with a fully automated, web-accessible solution. LecSched integrates a greedy randomized scheduling algorithm with a modern React-based front-end, a Node.js / Express back-end, and a cloud-hosted PostgreSQL database, providing a complete timetable management platform accessible from any device with a web browser.

1.2 Statement of the Problem

At many universities, the process of creating lecture timetables remains largely manual. Departmental secretaries or timetabling officers collect course offerings from each department, match them to available lecturers, and then attempt to fill a weekly grid of days and time slots while ensuring that no two courses share the same room or lecturer at the same time. This process suffers from several well-documented shortcomings:

  1. Time Consumption: A complete semester timetable can take days or weeks to assemble manually, especially when constraints change mid-semester. The effort is repeated each academic session with no meaningful reuse of previous schedules.
  2. Human Error: Manual grid-filling is inherently unreliable at scale. With hundreds of course sections, it is virtually impossible for a human planner to guarantee the absence of clashes without systematic verification.
  3. Inflexibility to Change: When a lecturer is reassigned, a room becomes unavailable, or a new course offering is added, the entire schedule may need to be manually reworked, causing administrative bottlenecks.
  4. Lack of Transparency: Paper-based or spreadsheet-based schedules are difficult to distribute and often become outdated quickly. Lecturers may receive conflicting versions, and there is no single authoritative, up-to-date source.
  5. No Scenario Planning: Manual processes do not support rapid generation of alternative schedules. Administrators cannot easily compare different timetabling strategies (e.g., scheduling all computer science courses first vs. interleaving across departments).
  6. Poor Reporting: There is no automated way to produce reports on room utilization, lecturer workload, or timetable quality (clash rates) without manual tallying.

These problems collectively lead to scheduling conflicts that disrupt academic activities, frustrate both lecturers and students, and reduce the overall efficiency of university operations. There is therefore a clear need for a computerized system that automates the timetable generation process, enforces constraints programmatically, and provides a centralized, accessible platform for schedule management.

1.3 Aim and Objectives

Aim

The aim of this project is to design, develop, and deploy a web-based Automated Lecture Timetable Generation System (LecSched) that eliminates manual scheduling conflicts, reduces the time required to produce a timetable, and provides a centralized platform for academic schedule management.

This study focuses on the automated generation of weekly lecture timetables for a university environment. The system covers the following functional areas:

  • The system does not currently support student-level scheduling (e.g., student timetable personalization or course registration integration).

  • Room-type preferences are limited to lectures and labs. More granular preferences (e.g., projector requirement, proximity to a specific building) are not supported.
  • The chat module does not support file attachments or group video conferencing.
  • Only the admin role can generate timetables; lecturers can only view and export them.

2.2 Conceptual Framework / Theoretical Background

2.2.1 The University Timetabling Problem

The university timetabling problem (UTP) is a well-studied combinatorial optimization problem in operations research and computer science. It involves assigning a set of events (lectures, tutorials, labs) to a set of time slots and rooms such that a collection of hard and soft constraints are satisfied (Burke & Petrovic, 2002).

Formally, the UTP can be described as follows. Given:

  • A set of courses C = {c1, c2, ..., cn}, each with an associated number of students and course type (lecture, lab, tutorial).
  • A set of lecturers L = {l1, l2, ..., lm}, each potentially available for a subset of the courses.
  • A set of rooms R = {r1, r2, ..., rp}, each with a capacity and a type (lecture hall, lab, seminar room).
  • A set of time slots T = {t1, t2, ..., tq} arranged over a weekly timetable grid (typically 5 weekdays × multiple periods per day).

The goal is to find a mapping f: C → T × R that assigns each course to a time slot and room such that all hard constraints are satisfied and a measure of soft constraint satisfaction is maximized.

2.2.2 Constraint Classification

Scheduling constraints in the UTP are broadly classified as:

  • Hard Constraints: Must be satisfied in any feasible timetable. Examples include:
    • No room clash: two courses cannot share the same room at the same time.
    • No lecturer clash: a lecturer cannot teach two courses simultaneously.
    • No group clash: courses assigned to the same group of students cannot overlap in time.
    • Room capacity: the room must accommodate the number of students enrolled.
  • Soft Constraints: Desirable but not mandatory. Examples include:
    • Preference for certain time slots (e.g., morning vs. afternoon).
    • Room-type preference (e.g., a course preferring a specific building).
    • Lecturer workload balancing across the week.
    • Minimizing gaps in students' daily schedules.

2.2.3 Computational Complexity

The UTP is known to be NP-hard in its general form (Garey & Johnson, 1979). This means that as the problem size grows (more courses, rooms, and constraints), finding an optimal solution becomes computationally intractable within polynomial time. Consequently, most practical timetabling systems rely on heuristic and meta-heuristic approaches that produce near-optimal solutions within reasonable time limits (Babaei & Abaspur, 2020).

2.2.4 Approaches to Timetabling

The literature identifies several categories of solution approaches:

  1. Exact Methods: Integer Linear Programming (ILP), Constraint Programming (CP). These guarantee optimality but scale poorly (Dorne & Hao, 1998).
  2. Heuristics: Greedy constructive heuristics, hill-climbing. Fast but may get trapped in local optima.
  3. Meta-heuristics: Simulated Annealing (SA), Genetic Algorithms (GA), Tabu Search (TS), Ant Colony Optimization (ACO). These explore the solution space more broadly and are the most widely used for university timetabling (Abdullah & Sahib, 2021).
  4. Hybrid Approaches: Combine constructive heuristics with meta-heuristic refinement to balance solution quality and computation time.

2.3 Review of Related Concepts (Key Technologies, Algorithms, and Models)

2.3.1 Greedy Randomized Heuristic

A greedy constructive heuristic builds a timetable incrementally, making a locally optimal assignment at each step (e.g., placing the most constrained course first). The randomized variant introduces controlled randomness (e.g., shuffling the order of candidate days, rooms, or time slots) to produce different solutions on successive runs. This diversity enables the system to explore multiple scheduling configurations and select the best, without the complexity of full meta-heuristic frameworks (Tsai & Kao, 2005).

In this project, the scheduling algorithm sorts course offerings by credit hours (descending), then iterates through shuffled days and time blocks to find feasible room assignments for each course. Three tracking structures (lecturerBusy, classroomBusy, groupCells) enforce hard constraints in real-time.

2.3.2 Relational Database Modeling

University timetabling data is inherently relational. Entities such as faculties, departments, programmes, courses, lecturers, and rooms are connected through well-defined one-to-many and many-to-many relationships. A normalized relational database (in this case, PostgreSQL) ensures data integrity, efficient querying, and support for complex joins required during timetable generation (Date, 2019).

The Entity-Relationship (ER) model is used to design the database schema. In this project, 17 Sequelize models define the tables and their associations, including Faculty→Department→Program→AcademicLevel→CourseOffering→TimetableSlot chains.

2.3.3 RESTful API Architecture

Representational State Transfer (REST) is the dominant architectural style for web APIs. RESTful APIs use standard HTTP methods (GET, POST, PUT, DELETE) to perform CRUD operations on resources identified by URLs. The LecSched back-end exposes 19 route modules under the /api prefix, each implementing RESTful endpoints for its respective entity (Fielding, 2000).

2.3.4 JWT-Based Authentication

JSON Web Tokens (JWT) provide a stateless authentication mechanism. Upon login, the server issues a signed token containing the user's identity and role. Subsequent requests include this token in the Authorization header, and the server verifies it without maintaining session state. JWT is particularly well-suited for serverless deployments (such as Vercel) where request-level isolation prevents traditional session storage (Jones et al., 2015).

2.3.5 WebSocket-Based Real-Time Communication

Socket.IO extends the WebSocket protocol with automatic fallback to HTTP long-polling and provides features such as rooms, namespaces, and reconnection handling. It is used in LecSched to implement a real-time chat system with public rooms and private messaging between users (Bankar, 2023).

2.3.6 Single-Page Application (SPA) Architecture

Modern web applications increasingly adopt SPA architecture, where a single HTML page loads a JavaScript bundle that handles all client-side routing and rendering dynamically. React, the most widely used SPA framework, enables component-based development, virtual DOM diffing for efficient updates, and a rich ecosystem of libraries. LecSched's front-end is built with React 19 and Vite, providing fast development iteration and optimized production builds (Meta, 2023).

2.3.7 Serverless Deployment

Serverless platforms such as Vercel allow back-end code to be deployed as individual functions that execute in response to HTTP requests. This eliminates the need for explicit server management and provides automatic scaling. Vercel's serverless functions support Node.js, making it straightforward to deploy an Express application (Vercel, 2024).

2.4 Review of Related / Existing Systems

2.4.1 FET Timetable Software

FET (Free Timetabling Software) is an open-source, cross-platform application for automatically scheduling university timetables. It uses a constraint-based approach with a heuristic algorithm and supports an extensive set of constraint types (soft and hard). FET is desktop-based, written in C++/Qt, and is widely used in European universities. However, it is not web-based and requires local installation (FET, 2024).

2.4.2 University Timetabler (UT)

University Timetabler is a commercial web-based timetabling system used by several UK universities. It provides an intuitive graphical interface, constraint definition tools, and reporting features. While powerful, it is a paid product and offers limited customization for institutions with unique scheduling requirements (University Timetabler, 2024).

2.4.3 TimeTabler

TimeTabler is one of the longest-established timetabling tools, developed for UK further and higher education. It offers manual, assisted, and automatic timetabling modes, with a powerful clash resolution system. It is a Windows desktop application with a proprietary license, making it inaccessible to institutions with limited budgets (TimeTabler, 2024).

2.4.4 UniTime

UniTime is a web-based timetabling platform developed at Purdue University and distributed under an open-source license. It supports course timetabling, examination timetabling, and student scheduling. Built with Java and backed by a relational database, it is one of the most feature-rich open-source options. However, its Java-based architecture requires significant server infrastructure, and its user interface is considered dated compared to modern web applications (UniTime, 2024).

2.4.5 Google Calendar / Outlook Integration

While not dedicated timetabling systems, calendar applications such as Google Calendar and Microsoft Outlook are sometimes used by lecturers to manually create their weekly schedules. These tools provide notification and sharing features but lack any constraint enforcement or automated generation capability. They are not suitable as institutional timetabling solutions.

2.6 Gaps Identified in Literature

Based on the review of existing systems and approaches, the following gaps are identified:

  1. Lack of Affordable Web-Based Solutions for Developing-World Universities: Most existing automated timetabling systems are either desktop-based (FET, TimeTabler), require significant server infrastructure (UniTime), or are commercial products (University Timetabler) beyond the reach of under-resourced institutions.
  2. No Integrated Communication Layer: Existing systems treat timetabling as a standalone function. None of the surveyed systems include built-in real-time communication features, which are valuable for coordinating schedule changes and resolving conflicts.
  3. Complex Setup and Maintenance: Java-based systems (UniTime) require dedicated server administration. Desktop applications (FET, TimeTabler) require installation on individual machines and do not support centralized access.
  4. Limited Mobile Support: Several existing systems (FET, TimeTabler) are not accessible from mobile devices, despite the growing reliance on smartphones and tablets among academic staff.
  5. No Modern Deployment Models: None of the existing systems leverage serverless cloud deployment (e.g., Vercel, AWS Lambda), which would eliminate infrastructure management and provide automatic scaling.
  6. Separation of Management and Generation: In many systems, data entry (courses, rooms, lecturers) and timetable generation are handled by different tools or separate interfaces, creating workflow discontinuity.

LecSched addresses these gaps by providing an integrated, web-based, serverless-deployed timetabling platform that combines entity management, automated generation, visual display, export, and real-time communication in a single application.

2.7 Summary of Literature Review

This chapter established the theoretical foundations of the university timetabling problem, classified it as an NP-hard combinatorial optimization problem, and surveyed the major solution approaches (exact methods, heuristics, meta-heuristics). The key enabling technologies underpinning the proposed system — relational databases, RESTful APIs, JWT authentication, WebSockets, SPAs, and serverless deployment — were reviewed. An analysis of five existing timetabling systems revealed that while capable solutions exist, they are either not web-based, not free, require complex infrastructure, or lack integrated communication features. The identified gaps justify the design and development of LecSched as an accessible, integrated, and modern solution to university lecture timetabling.

3.3 Analysis of the Existing System

The existing timetabling process at the institution was studied through observation and interviews with departmental administrative staff. The process can be described as follows:

3.3.1 Current Process Flow

  1. Data Collection: Each department submits a list of courses to be offered in the upcoming semester, along with the number of students and preferred lecturers. These are typically communicated via email or paper memos.
  2. Resource Compilation: The timetabling officer compiles a list of available rooms (with capacity and type information) and available time blocks.
  3. Schedule Assembly: Using a spreadsheet (Microsoft Excel) or a paper-based grid, the officer manually places each course into a day-and-time cell, checking for clashes by visual inspection.
  4. Conflict Resolution: When clashes are detected (two courses in the same room or the same lecturer assigned to two courses simultaneously), the officer manually rearranges entries until the clash is resolved.
  5. Distribution: The completed schedule is printed and distributed to departments. Updated versions are distributed as corrections are made, sometimes via email.

3.3.2 Users of the Existing System

  • Timetabling Officer / Departmental Secretary: Primary operator; compiles and maintains the schedule.
  • Head of Department: Reviews and approves the departmental schedule.
  • Lecturers: Receive the printed schedule and follow it for class delivery.
  • Dean / Faculty Administrator: Oversees scheduling across multiple departments within a faculty.

3.5 Analysis of the Proposed System

3.5.1 Overview

LecSched is a web-based system that automates the entire timetable lifecycle: from managing academic entities (faculties, departments, programmes, courses) to generating conflict-free schedules, displaying them in an interactive grid, and exporting them for distribution. The system is accessible from any modern web browser, on both desktop and mobile devices, without requiring software installation.

3.5.2 Proposed Process Flow

  1. System Setup (One-Time): The administrator logs in and configures the system by defining faculties, departments, programmes, academic levels, academic years, semesters, buildings, floors, classrooms, and time slots.
  2. Course and Offering Setup (Per Semester): The administrator adds courses to their respective departments and programmes, then creates course offerings by linking each course to an academic level, semester, and lecturer.
  3. Timetable Generation: The administrator selects the target semester (and optionally a faculty or department filter) and clicks "Generate Timetable." The system runs the scheduling algorithm and produces a complete, clash-free timetable.
  4. Review and Export: The administrator reviews the generated schedule in the visual timetable viewer, applies filters (by programme or level), and exports the timetable as a PDF or JSON file.
  5. Ongoing Management: The administrator can regenerate the timetable when constraints change (new course added, lecturer reassigned). Generation history is logged for audit purposes.
  6. Communication: Lecturers and administrators use the built-in chat system to discuss scheduling issues in real time.

4.5 Program/Module Design (Pseudocode, Flowcharts, Algorithms)

4.5.1 Greedy Randomized Scheduling Algorithm

The core of the system is the timetable-generation algorithm. It is a greedy, randomised algorithm that places course offerings into free time blocks in eligible classrooms while respecting lecturer availability and avoiding double-booking. Its flow was introduced in Chapter Three (Figure 3.4). The algorithm is summarised below in structured pseudocode.

ALGORITHM: GenerateTimetable(semesterId, departmentFilter) INPUT: semesterId - the target semester departmentFilter - optional department restriction OUTPUT: list of placed TimetableSlots (with clashes reported) 1. VALIDATE that the semester exists. 2. LOAD all course offerings for semesterId. IF departmentFilter provided, FILTER offerings by department. 3. LOAD all active time slots (class blocks) and all classrooms. 4. If no offerings OR no time slots OR no classrooms: RETURN error "insufficient data to generate". 5. INITIALISE tracking data structures: groupMap := map[program+level] -> list of offerings roomBusy := map[classroomId+day+block] -> offering lecBusy := map[lecturerId+day+block] -> offering 6. FOR each (program, level) group in groupMap: a. SORT offerings in the group by credit hours (descending). b. FOR each offering in the group: i. SHUFFLE the list of days (Fisher-Yates). ii. FOR each day: iii. SHUFFLE the list of time blocks. iv. FOR each block: - IF lecturer is already busy (lecBusy) THEN record clash, CONTINUE. - SHUFFLE the list of classrooms. - FOR each classroom: IF room busy (roomBusy) THEN CONTINUE. IF room type does not match course type THEN CONTINUE. IF room capacity < numStudents THEN CONTINUE. PLACE offering in (day, block, room). UPDATE roomBusy and lecBusy. BREAK to next offering. - IF no room found for block, try next block. v. IF not placed after all days, record as unplaced. 7. RETURN the set of placed slots and any clashes/unplaced counts.

4.7 Implementation Details (Key Code Snippets Explained)

This section highlights several important implementation decisions, including the authentication flow, the avatar upload fix, and the scheduling engine. Representative code fragments are provided with explanations.

4.7.1 Statistical (Stateless) JWT Authentication

Authentication is implemented using JSON Web Tokens. On login, the server verifies the user’s credentials with Bcrypt and returns a signed token containing the user id and role. Every subsequent request is guarded by an authentication middleware that verifies the token present in the Authorization header. The token is stored in the client’s memory (React context) and attached automatically by an Axios interceptor.

4.7.2 Avatar Upload Resolution (Settings Profile Update)

During user acceptance testing, updating the profile photo on the Settings page failed with HTTP 413 (payload too large). The default Express JSON body limit is 100 KB, which was insufficient for base64-encoded avatar images. Two coordinated fixes were applied:

  • Server: The Express body-parser limit was raised to 5 MB (express.json({ limit: '5mb' })), and the PUT /auth/me handler was enhanced to re-issue a fresh JWT after a profile update and to check email uniqueness to prevent duplicate emails.
  • Client: The avatar is now compressed and resized client-side (maximum dimension 512 px, JPEG quality 0.85) before upload using a canvas-based resizing routine. This keeps the payload small and the upload fast.

4.7.3 Scheduling Engine Implementation

The placement logic is implemented as a self-contained loop. Rather than issuing a database query per placement (which is slow and inefficient), the engine loads the relevant data once into memory and uses two JavaScript Map objects (roomBusy and lecBusy) to track occupancy. After all placements are decided, the results are committed to the database in a single bulk insert, followed by logging the run to the GenerationHistories table. This approach yields an execution time of only a few milliseconds even for large semesters.

4.7.4 Demo Data Seeding

To support a live demonstration and evaluation, a comprehensive seed script (server/seed-demo.js) was written. It populates the database with four faculties, ten departments, eleven programmes, 42 academic levels, two academic years, two semesters, seven buildings, 22 classrooms, 14 lecturers, 42 courses, 40 course offerings, 76 timetable slots, 56 lecturer schedules, and 12 chat messages. The seed data was carefully verified via SQL to contain zero same-semester classroom clashes, zero lecturer clashes, and zero level-overlap clashes.

4.9 System Requirements for Deployment (Installation, Environment Setup)

4.9.1 Local Development Setup

Because the project is an npm monorepo with workspaces, both the client and server are managed together. The following dependencies must be installed on the development machine: Node.js (version 20 or higher) and npm (version 10 or higher). The project is cloned from the Git repository, dependencies are installed at the root, and a .env file is created from the provided template containing the database connection string and JWT secret. The application is then started in development mode, which launches both the Vite dev server for the client and the Express server for the API simultaneously.

4.9.2 Production Deployment on Vercel

Deployment to Vercel is automatic and zero-configuration:

  1. The repository is linked to a Vercel project.
  2. Environment variables (database URL, JWT secret, etc.) are configured in the Vercel dashboard.
  3. On every push to the main branch, Vercel builds the static React application and deploys the Express API as serverless functions.
  4. The Neon PostgreSQL database is provisioned separately and connected via the pooled connection string.

The database schema is synchronised automatically through the Sequelize sync() mechanism at startup, and demo data can be loaded by running the seed script against the production database.

CHAPTER FIVE

SUMMARY, CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS

5.1 Summary of the Study

The aim of this project was to design and implement a web-based lecture timetable management system to address the recurring difficulties lecturers and administrators face in manually compiling timetables. Manual scheduling is time-consuming, prone to double-booking and conflicts, difficult to maintain, and hard to distribute. The delivered system, termed LecSched, automates the detection and resolution of such conflicts through an efficient scheduling algorithm.

The study began with a review of the problem background, identified the specific problems inherent in manual scheduling, and set out clear aims, objectives, and scope (Chapter One). A review of related literature and similar systems established the theoretical and technological foundation (Chapter Two), and a detailed system analysis produced functional and non-functional requirements, use case, class, sequence, and activity diagrams, together with hardware and software requirements (Chapter Three). Chapter Four described the full design and implementation of the working system, including the database schema, input/output design, the scheduling algorithm, and the testing carried out. This final chapter summarises the project, highlights its achievements, discusses challenges, draws conclusions, and offers recommendations and suggestions for future enhancement.

5.2 Achievements / Contributions of the Project

The project successfully achieved all of the objectives set out in Chapter One. The principal achievements are:

  • Automated conflict-free scheduling: A greedy randomized algorithm that places course offerings into free time blocks and eligible classrooms while tracking both room and lecturer occupancy, producing schedules with zero same-semester clashes in the demo data.
  • Comprehensive management module: Full CRUD management for faculties, departments, programmes, academic years, semesters, levels, courses, course offerings, lecturers, buildings, classrooms, and time slots.
  • Role-based access control: Secure JWT-based authentication distinguishing administrators from lecturers, with each role granted appropriate permissions.
  • Export functionality: The timetable can be exported in both PDF (landscape A4 grid) and JSON formats for distribution and interoperability.
  • Analytics and reporting: An interactive dashboard and reports visualising slot distribution, lecturer workload, and departmental activity via charts.
  • Real-time communication: A Socket.IO-based chat supporting private and group messages between administrators and lecturers.
  • Cloud deployment: A modern full-stack application deployed to the Vercel platform with a managed PostgreSQL database on Neon, demonstrating production-readiness.

5.3 Challenges Encountered

Several challenges were encountered and addressed during the development of the system:

  • Classroom resource constraints: Limited classroom capacity and room types (lecture halls, labs, seminar rooms) required careful matching between the room type/capacity and each course’s requirements during placement.
  • Conflict avoidance complexity: Ensuring no two offerings occupied the same classroom, lecturer, or academic level simultaneously required the algorithm to maintain and consult multiple occupancy tracking structures.
  • Avatar upload failure (HTTP 413): Uploading profile images exceeded the default Express JSON body limit. This was resolved by raising the server body limit and compressing images client-side before upload.
  • Environment configuration: Coordinating the monorepo build, serverless deployment, and cloud database connection variables required careful configuration across the Vercel environment.
  • Demo data integrity: Constructing a realistic, comprehensive demo dataset that was simultaneously clash-free and sufficient to demonstrate all features demanded iterative verification against the database.

5.4 Conclusion

The project set out to eliminate the inefficiencies and errors of manual lecture timetable compilation. It has successfully delivered a complete, functional, and deployable web-based lecture timetable management system. The system automates the otherwise tedious and error-prone scheduling process, enforces conflict-free placement, centralises timetable management, provides convenient export options, and offers real-time communication among staff. Through the application of a greedy randomized scheduling algorithm, the system generates valid timetables in milliseconds, far exceeding the speed and reliability of manual methods.

The system was tested thoroughly and all test cases passed, including the resolution of the profile-update defect discovered during acceptance testing. The final product is not only a practical tool but also a demonstration of the effective integration of modern technologies including React, Express, Sequelize, PostgreSQL, Socket.IO, and serverless cloud deployment. It is therefore concluded that the objectives of the project have been fully met and that LecSched provides a robust, scalable foundation for lecture timetable management in a university setting.

5.5 Recommendations

Based on the outcomes of this project, the following recommendations are made:

  • Academic institutions that currently rely on manual or spreadsheet-based scheduling should adopt automated systems such as LecSched to improve accuracy, save staff time, and reduce scheduling conflicts.
  • Institutions should ensure adequate, well-catalogued classroom resources (with accurate capacities and types) are available so that automated algorithms can produce optimal schedules.
  • Administrators should periodically review and update lecturer availability preferences so that generated schedules respect the teaching staff’s constraints.
  • Institutions should provide training to administrative and academic staff on the use of such systems to maximise their benefits.

5.6 Suggestions for Future Work

Although the system fully meets its objectives, several enhancements could be made in future iterations:

  • Optimised scheduling algorithms: Integrating genetic algorithms or simulated annealing could yield more optimal solutions when handling very large numbers of offerings or stricter constraints.
  • Student-facing timetables: Extending the system to allow student registrations so that each student can view their personalised weekly timetable.
  • Manual adjustment: Adding a drag-and-drop interface that lets administrators manually adjust individual slots after automatic generation while retaining conflict detection.
  • Email/SMS notifications: Automatically notifying lecturers of their timetable via email or SMS on generation or change.
  • Multi-campus and multi-session support: Supporting timetables across multiple campuses and both full-time and part-time (evening/weekend) sessions.
  • Exam scheduling: Extending the scheduling engine to handle end-of-semester examination timetables as well as regular lectures.
